01 · The question

Start with the decision, not the dataset.

The point was not to build the most complicated model. It was to help decide where a county-level program could make a meaningful first move, who it could reach, and what assumptions sat behind that recommendation. County was the operating unit because it supports broader outreach and can simplify coordination through local health departments and community resources.

01

Where should it launch?

Rank counties by a blend of food-access burden, vulnerable populations, and related health measures.

02

How many people are in scope?

Use the low-access population as the addressable group, then separate reach from a hypothetical engagement scenario.

03

Which signals deserve attention?

Track low-income residents with low access, children with low access, and low-access households without a vehicle.

02 · Method

Two public datasets, one county-level shortlist.

The analysis links USDA county food-access measures to health estimates from census tracts covered by CDC 500 Cities, converts percentage-based health measures into approximate counts, and rolls the matched tracts up to the county level.

1

Load

Read the Food Environment Atlas and 500 Cities tables from SQLite into pandas.

2

Join

Standardize FIPS fields and match census tracts to their five-digit county code.

3

Score

Apply a 15-variable reach score, with more weight on direct access measures than health context.

4

Rank

Aggregate tract estimates, rescore the matched counties, and inspect the highest-ranked locations.

Access + demographic signals

72 pts

Six measures cover low access overall and among low-income residents, households without vehicles, SNAP households, children, and seniors.

Health context + population change

50 pts

Eight health estimates contribute five percentage points each; population change contributes ten. The original stated weights total 122%, so the score is best read as a ranking index, not a percentage.

Variable selection

Choose for the decision, then test the choice.

The notebook uses a manual, purpose-led screen: keep absolute 2015 counts because the goal is to estimate how many people a program could reach; retain population change for direction; drop percentage versions when equivalent counts are already present; and use health measures as context rather than direct evidence of food access.

How importance would be tested next: remove fields with high missingness or duplicate meaning, normalize every measure to a comparable scale, check correlations to avoid double-counting, and rerun the ranking while dropping or reweighting one variable at a time. The strongest variables would be the ones that remain stable, interpretable, and directly tied to decisions a food-access program can make.

03 · Results

Three large counties stand out within the matched sample.

The ranking covers counties represented after joining USDA data to CDC 500 Cities tracts, not every U.S. county or every tract within each county. It favors large absolute counts of people facing low store access, which is useful for finding scale but is not a per-capita measure of severity.

04 · Takeaway

A ranking is a place to start, not the answer.

The model gets a broad, messy problem down to a shortlist within the data it covers. The next step would be pressure-testing that shortlist with local knowledge, current data, implementation cost, and partner capacity.

“The biggest lesson was learning to separate reach from need. A county can offer scale without having the highest per-capita burden.”

What I would improve next

  • Normalize inputs before weighting them.
  • Test how sensitive the ranking is to each weight.
  • Account for overlap among population subgroups.
  • Replace the 45% scenario with a locally validated uptake range.
  • Add cost, delivery capacity, and current conditions.
